# Phase 2: do delayed discharges explain the A&E waits?

**Question:** The Phase 1 memo said the A&E data shows *what* is happening, not *why*. Public Health Scotland also publishes delayed-discharge data (patients medically ready to leave hospital but still in a bed). Does it explain which boards struggle?

**Scope:** 14 health boards, July 2016 to August 2026. A&E figures are Type 1 EDs. Delay figures are adults aged 18+, "All Delay Reasons" only, Scotland total excluded. "Latest 12 months" = Sep 2025 to Aug 2026. Source: Public Health Scotland open data (OGL v3.0).

## Findings

1. **Blocked beds have grown by about a third.** Average daily delayed beds rose from about 1,455 (2019) to about 1,958 (latest 12 months). Delayed bed days rose from 530,886 to 714,462. Per 1,000 Type 1 A&E attendances: 369 to 493.
2. **It is structural, not seasonal.** Across 2022 onward, the national average runs from about 1,835 (April to June) to about 1,960 (November). The winter swing is small compared with the overall level.
3. **Delayed discharge does not explain the gap between boards.** Across the 14 boards the link is close to zero (rank correlation 0.12; 0.0 once the four smallest boards are removed). Lanarkshire has the lowest delayed-bed rate in Scotland (296 per 1,000 attendances) yet only 52.0% seen within 4 hours. Western Isles has the highest (1,564) yet 91.9% within 4 hours. Small boards have few beds, so their rates swing, which is part of why the comparison is noisy.
4. **The change since 2019 tells a slightly stronger but still mixed story.** Boards where delays rose most tended to see bigger A&E falls (correlation 0.44 across 14 boards, which is weak evidence with so few boards). But Lanarkshire (-33.8 points), Fife (-28.1) and Lothian (-21.0) all fell sharply while their delayed-bed rates went *down*.
5. **Within a board, months with more delays than usual tended to have worse A&E results** (correlation -0.27, 2022 onward, board averages removed). Modest, and trends over time can also drive this.
6. **The mix of reasons has shifted.** "Code 9 - adults with incapacity (AWI)" delayed bed days doubled (73,609 to 147,828), moving from 13.9% to 20.7% of all delayed bed days. Health and social care reasons are still the largest (69.3%, down from 77.8%). Patient and family reasons are small (3.6%).

## So what

- Delayed discharge is a real and growing national pressure (about 500 more blocked beds a day than in 2019), and worth its own recommendation.
- It is **not** the main explanation for why Forth Valley, Lanarkshire and Grampian are worst. For those boards the cause is more likely to sit inside the hospital: ED flow, admission beds, staffing, or site-level capacity. This dataset cannot confirm that.
- This changes Phase 1 recommendation 5. Discharge data is now added and is not enough on its own. Total bed capacity and staffing are still the missing layer.

## Suggested additions to the recommendations

- Treat discharge delays as a national workstream (about 500 more blocked beds than in 2019), with special attention to the doubling of adults-with-incapacity delays.
- Do not assume fixing discharge alone will close the A&E gap in the worst boards.
- Next data layers: total hospital beds and staffing by board.

## Limitations

- Adults 18+ only for delays; A&E includes children.
- 14 data points for the board comparison, so correlations are indicative, not conclusive. Correlation is not cause.
- Delayed bed days are scaled by Type 1 attendances, not by population or total beds, so small boards are noisy.
- Delay figures are at board of delay, not by hospital site.
